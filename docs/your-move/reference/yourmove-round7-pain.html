4 Undo a ruling

The pain: accepting too fast — the misclick, the skimmed evidence. "Accept" is currently forever, which makes every ruling heavier than it needs to be.

The withdrawal is a compensating event — history isn't rewritten. The task is your move again; the evidence drops back to recorded.

  • U160 seconds, then it's history. The window is short on purpose — undo is for misclicks, not second thoughts. After it lapses, the ruling stands like any other.
  • U2An optional reason travels with it — "skimmed the evidence" becomes part of the trail. Future-you learns from it.
  • U3The event log makes this honest by default. No other undo mechanism can show both the ruling and its withdrawal in order. This is the architecture showing off.

5 Attested acceptance

The pain: accepting without really looking — the trust half of the job has no ceremony, so "checked" quietly degrades into "glanced at".

The attestation is recorded with the ruling — your name is on the specifics, not just the outcome.

  • A1Ceremony, not friction. No checkbox, no modal — the statement sits above the button and the ruling records it. Reading it is the ceremony.
  • A2Generated from the evidence states, not written by hand. If only 1 of 3 is checked, the attestation says so — it can't flatter the review.
  • A3Strongest where it matters most: "Accept anyway" on failed verification gets the longest attestation — the override names exactly what was overridden.

7 Ruling receipts

The receipt states the effect, not just the act: who was notified, what resumed, what stood. The trail keeps it permanently.

  • R1The toast grows up. Every ruling's confirmation names its effect — notified whom, resumed what, what stood. The act and its consequence travel together.
  • R2Reasons ride along. "Sent back — 'line 14 doesn't foot'" — the one-line reason lands in the trail, so the worker (and future-you) knows why.
  • R3Pairs with the journey. The ghost flight shows the trip; the receipt states the landing. Motion and words, same truth.
